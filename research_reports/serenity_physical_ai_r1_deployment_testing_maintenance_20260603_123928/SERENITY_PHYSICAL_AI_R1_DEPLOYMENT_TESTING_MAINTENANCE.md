## 1. Executive Summary

Deployment/testing/maintenance is the strongest Physical AI bottleneck because it is where robotics leaves the demo surface and becomes a working physical system. Robot OEMs can ship hardware, and AI vendors can publish models, but customers only pay at scale when a site can be surveyed, safety-designed, installed, commissioned, calibrated, maintained and proven against ROI targets.

This lane is less hype than humanoid demos because it already has hard anchors: industrial robots have a large installed base, warehouse automation has disclosed fleet-scale deployments and filings-backed backlog/revenue evidence, and updated robot safety standards explicitly cover integration, installation and safeguarding. The bottleneck is therefore not a claim that all humanoids will work; it is the dull conversion layer that decides whether any robot fleet can remain productive and safe.

What would make this lane investable later:
- repeat deployments tied to named customers;
- service revenue or backlog converting into revenue;
- installation throughput and acceptance-test evidence;
- safety certification and validation demand that cannot be commoditized;
- spare-parts, uptime monitoring or field-service economics visible in filings or segment data;
- supplier concentration, qualification lock-in or installed-base attachment.

What remains unproven:
- whether humanoid pilots become broad repeat deployments;
- whether service and maintenance economics are attractive after labor, spares and warranty costs;
- whether integration is durable pricing power or local project labor;
- whether public-company exposure is pure enough, since many suppliers are diversified or private;
- whether warehouse automation backlog converts to margin and cash flow outside concentrated customer relationships.

## 2. Supply-Chain Stack For This Lane

1. **robot OEM / automation system** -> Robot arm, AMR, AS/RS, sortation, humanoid pilot or full warehouse automation system | bottleneck: Customer task exists but does not yet prove deployment scalability | evidence: E2-E5 depending on system
2. **system integrator** -> Turns robot/equipment into a working cell/site workflow | bottleneck: Skilled integrators, project management and local support scale slowly | evidence: E3-E4
3. **site survey / safety design** -> Risk assessment, layout, guarding, traffic flow, human-robot interaction design | bottleneck: Each site is different; safety cannot be copied blindly | evidence: E3
4. **deployment engineering** -> Installation, commissioning, workflow integration, worker training, acceptance tests | bottleneck: Labor-intensive and customer-specific; delays can bottleneck revenue conversion | evidence: E3-E4
5. **industrial controls / safety systems** -> PLCs, safety controllers, safety scanners, drives, real-time networks | bottleneck: Functional safety and deterministic control are hard to retrofit | evidence: E3-E4
6. **calibration / QA / testing** -> Robot calibration, vision setup, metrology, acceptance tests, process QA | bottleneck: Robots drift, sensors change, sites vary, and uncalibrated automation creates scrap/downtime | evidence: E3-E4
7. **field service / spare parts** -> Technician network, repairs, preventive maintenance, parts warehouses | bottleneck: Downtime cost creates urgency; parts and trained technicians become chokepoints | evidence: E4-E5
8. **uptime monitoring / service software** -> Predictive maintenance, remote monitoring, diagnostics, fleet health, alerts | bottleneck: Fleet scale requires monitoring and service triage, not manual firefighting | evidence: E3-E4
9. **customer ROI proof** -> Throughput, labor savings, uptime, safety incidents, payback, cash conversion | bottleneck: Customers stop adopting if ROI is not measurable after deployment | evidence: E4-E5

## 3. Supplier-Category Map


### System integrator
- What supplier does: Designs and delivers robot/automation cells or warehouse systems; owns layout, controls, commissioning, acceptance and handoff.
- Why bottlenecked: Bottlenecked by skilled labor, project execution, local support, safety competence and customer-specific workflow complexity.
- Evidence needed: Completed deployments; certified integrator status; repeat customers; backlog converted to installations; acceptance-test data.
- Public-company mapping difficulty: Medium: many integrators are private or buried inside larger automation companies.
- Likely public-company types: automation integrators; warehouse automation system houses; industrial controls service groups; engineering services firms.
- No-go warning signs: large partner logos without completed sites; fixed-price projects with overruns; dependence on one customer; weak safety documentation.
- Current evidence ladder: E3-E4
- Serenity-style fit: High

### Safety certification and compliance
- What supplier does: Performs risk assessment, guarding design, CE/OSHA/ISO/ANSI compliance support, validation and safety file preparation.
- Why bottlenecked: Bottlenecked because robot cells are site-specific and updated standards raise the knowledge floor.
- Evidence needed: Standards mapping; safety validation records; named certified projects; recurring safety-services revenue; customer audits.
- Public-company mapping difficulty: Medium: safety suppliers are often segments inside larger controls/sensor companies.
- Likely public-company types: safety automation vendors; certification/test labs; industrial safety consultancies; sensor/safety-controller suppliers.
- No-go warning signs: generic compliance language; no standard references; no validation evidence; demo cells outside production.
- Current evidence ladder: E3
- Serenity-style fit: High

### Deployment engineering / commissioning
- What supplier does: Installs, commissions, configures and stabilizes robot systems at customer sites, including worker training and acceptance testing.
- Why bottlenecked: Bottlenecked by travel/site labor, installation windows, controls debugging and customer process variation.
- Evidence needed: Installations per period; time-to-commission; acceptance test pass rates; rework rates; customer expansion after first site.
- Public-company mapping difficulty: Hard: deployment capacity is often inside OEM/project accounting, not reported separately.
- Likely public-company types: robot OEM service arms; warehouse automation project teams; industrial engineering contractors.
- No-go warning signs: revenue recognized before stable operation; repeated project delays; pilots without follow-on sites.
- Current evidence ladder: E3-E4
- Serenity-style fit: High

### Industrial controls / safety systems
- What supplier does: Provides PLCs, safety controllers, safety scanners, drives, I/O, industrial networks and safety logic.
- Why bottlenecked: Bottlenecked where safety-rated control and deterministic motion must be integrated with robots and mobile platforms.
- Evidence needed: Design-ins; product certification; installed-base service; safety-rated specs; integration with robot OEMs and standard protocols.
- Public-company mapping difficulty: Easy-to-medium: many suppliers are public large caps, but robot-specific exposure is harder to isolate.
- Likely public-company types: industrial automation; safety sensors; safety PLCs; motion-control vendors.
- No-go warning signs: commodity hardware with no safety differentiation; exposure too broad to isolate; unproven robot integration.
- Current evidence ladder: E3-E4
- Serenity-style fit: Medium-High

### Robot testing / QA / calibration
- What supplier does: Verifies robot accuracy, vision performance, metrology, calibration, traceability and production acceptance.
- Why bottlenecked: Bottlenecked because physical accuracy, sensor drift and process variation determine scrap/downtime after deployment.
- Evidence needed: Calibration cycles; acceptance-test standards; metrology attach rates; QA service revenue; reduced scrap/downtime case studies.
- Public-company mapping difficulty: Medium: metrology and machine vision suppliers are public, but service exposure can be opaque.
- Likely public-company types: metrology; machine vision; barcode/traceability; industrial inspection; test equipment.
- No-go warning signs: inspection demos not tied to production; accuracy claims without repeatability; poor service attachment.
- Current evidence ladder: E3-E4
- Serenity-style fit: Medium-High

### Field service / spare parts
- What supplier does: Maintains robots after go-live through preventive maintenance, repairs, spare parts, warranty, technician dispatch and remanufacturing.
- Why bottlenecked: Bottlenecked by installed-base growth, parts inventory, trained technicians and customer downtime urgency.
- Evidence needed: Installed base; service specialists; parts centers; service contracts; repair volumes; uptime metrics; spare-parts availability.
- Public-company mapping difficulty: Medium: OEMs disclose services but often not robot-only profitability.
- Likely public-company types: robot OEMs; industrial service networks; repair/remanufacturing specialists; aftermarket parts.
- No-go warning signs: no service footprint; inability to support old models; service revenue not scaling with installed base.
- Current evidence ladder: E4-E5
- Serenity-style fit: High

### Uptime monitoring / service software
- What supplier does: Monitors robot/fleet health, predicts failures, triggers maintenance and tracks uptime, alarms and consumables.
- Why bottlenecked: Bottlenecked because fleets cannot be supported manually once robot density rises.
- Evidence needed: Connected robots; predictive-maintenance adoption; reduced downtime; subscription/service revenue; integration with service dispatch.
- Public-company mapping difficulty: Medium: software may be bundled into OEM service contracts.
- Likely public-company types: industrial IoT; robot OEM service software; CMMS; condition monitoring; fleet orchestration.
- No-go warning signs: dashboard-only claims; no failure-rate reduction; no paid adoption; no service workflow integration.
- Current evidence ladder: E3-E4
- Serenity-style fit: Medium

### Warehouse automation ROI verification
- What supplier does: Measures throughput, labor productivity, error rates, uptime, installation cost, payback and customer expansion.
- Why bottlenecked: Bottlenecked because customers will not scale automation without visible economics and risk controls.
- Evidence needed: Revenue/backlog contribution; multi-customer adoption; same-customer expansion; cash conversion; site-level productivity metrics.
- Public-company mapping difficulty: Medium: public filings show revenue/backlog but often not site ROI.
- Likely public-company types: warehouse automation platforms; 3PL operators; WMS/WES software; controls and integration providers.
- No-go warning signs: single-customer backlog mistaken for broad demand; poor cash conversion; customer concentration; high service burden.
- Current evidence ladder: E4-E5
- Serenity-style fit: High

### Training / workforce enablement
- What supplier does: Trains technicians, operators and maintenance teams; provides procedures, support, documentation and change management.
- Why bottlenecked: Bottlenecked by shortage of robot-literate field technicians and operators as fleets scale.
- Evidence needed: Training programs; certified technicians; support response time; safety training; lower operator-caused downtime.
- Public-company mapping difficulty: Hard: often private or bundled in service organizations.
- Likely public-company types: robot OEM training; certified integrator training; industrial workforce platforms; technical education providers.
- No-go warning signs: claims of plug-and-play automation without training; high operator exception rate; unsafe interventions.
- Current evidence ladder: E2-E3
- Serenity-style fit: Medium

## 6. Disconfirmation Checklist

See `disconfirmation_checklist.csv` for row-level tests. Cross-cutting disconfirmers:
- integration capacity scales easily and cheaply with no certification bottleneck;
- customers can swap integrators or controls suppliers with little retraining or downtime;
- service labor and spare-parts costs consume the expected automation savings;
- deployments remain pilots and do not become repeat sites;
- backlog fails to convert to cash flow;
- uptime monitoring remains a dashboard feature, not paid failure reduction;
- safety standards become routine paperwork rather than a supplier-selection gate.

## 9. No-Go Areas

- Humanoid demo videos treated as adoption.
- Partner logos without repeat deployment, revenue or service burden disclosure.
- Integrator claims with no safety-standard mapping.
- Backlog without installation cadence, customer concentration and cash conversion analysis.
- Predictive maintenance dashboards without paid adoption or downtime reduction proof.
- Broad industrial automation companies where robot-specific exposure is too diluted to underwrite.
