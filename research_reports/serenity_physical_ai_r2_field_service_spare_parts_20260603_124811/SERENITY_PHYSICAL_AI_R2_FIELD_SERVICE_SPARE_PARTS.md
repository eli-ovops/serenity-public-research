## 1. Executive Summary

Field service / spare parts / uptime monitoring is a valid Physical AI bottleneck candidate because deployed robots are physical capital goods. They fail, drift, need lubrication, batteries, reducers, controllers, safety devices, calibration, software backups, spare parts and trained technicians. As robot density rises, customers do not only buy robots; they buy uptime.

This category is less hype than humanoid demos because the evidence starts from installed equipment, service networks, parts warehouses, safety standards and maintenance/support contracts. The strongest public evidence comes from mature industrial robots and adjacent warehouse/industrial automation infrastructure, not from general-purpose humanoid claims.

What is already confirmed:
- IFR confirms a large industrial robot installed base.
- KION filings show large service network scale, spare-parts relevance and installed-fleet service economics in adjacent industrial/warehouse equipment.
- Symbotic filings show software maintenance/support tied to operational systems, though with customer concentration risk.
- ISO/ANSI standards explicitly cover industrial robot lifecycle safety, including integration, commissioning, operation and maintenance.
- Public annual reports and official service pages show that major robot OEMs treat service, spare parts and uptime support as strategic offerings.

What remains unproven:
- robot-only service margin, renewal rate and cash-flow contribution are often not disclosed;
- many service economics are bundled inside larger OEM or industrial automation segments;
- predictive maintenance claims need more proof of paid adoption and downtime reduction;
- humanoid-specific service demand remains early and should not be assumed from industrial robot evidence;
- independent integrator service may commoditize unless certification, response time, proprietary access or customer lock-in is proven.

## 2. Category Value Chain

1. **robot installed base** -> Installed robots, AMRs, cobots, AS/RS, industrial trucks and warehouse automation cells create future maintenance obligations. Evidence to collect: fleet size, installed base, active systems, age mix, utilization intensity
2. **installed equipment monitoring** -> Robots and equipment produce alarms, utilization, condition, cycle and safety-state data. Evidence to collect: connected robots, fleet portals, alarm history, uptime metrics, paid monitoring attachment
3. **preventive maintenance** -> Scheduled inspections, lubrication, grease analysis, battery checks, reducer/servo checks and controller backup. Evidence to collect: service contracts, PM intervals, technician capacity, downtime reduction claims
4. **spare parts inventory** -> OEM or specialist stocks controllers, motors, reducers, cables, sensors, batteries and obsolete parts. Evidence to collect: parts count, warehouse network, dispatch speed, obsolete model support, parts revenue
5. **field technician dispatch** -> Qualified technicians diagnose, repair, update, recommission and support customer sites. Evidence to collect: service headcount, response times, locations, repair volumes, training/certifications
6. **calibration / recommissioning** -> After crashes, drift, tooling change or relocation, robots/sensors need calibration and acceptance checks. Evidence to collect: calibration services, metrology attach, recommissioning case studies, scrap/downtime reduction
7. **safety inspection** -> Safety scanners, cells, guarding, procedures and controls need validation over the lifecycle. Evidence to collect: ISO/ANSI linkage, inspection services, compliance records, safety validation revenue
8. **service contract renewal** -> Customers renew support, spare-parts, remote monitoring and maintenance contracts. Evidence to collect: renewal rates, attach rates, recurring revenue, support margins, contract duration
9. **customer uptime / ROI proof** -> Service proves itself by keeping automation productive and avoiding expensive downtime. Evidence to collect: uptime improvement, avoided downtime dollars, productivity metrics, payback, gross margin/cash conversion

## 4. Evidence Ladder For This Category

- **E0**: narrative only
- **E1**: demo / prototype
- **E2**: pilot / early deployment
- **E3**: named customer / repeat deployment
- **E4**: revenue or backlog contribution

For this R2 lane:

- E4 means service, spare parts, maintenance/support, aftersales, backlog, operational systems or contract revenue is visible in filings or formal disclosures.
- E5 means installed-base scale plus service network, repair volumes, parts infrastructure, renewal/margin/cash-flow evidence or hard uptime proof.

## 5. Supplier-Category Subsegments


### OEM service network
- What it does: Factory-authorized support, repair, preventive maintenance, lifetime maintenance, technician dispatch and warranty handling.
- Why it may bottleneck: Large installed bases create recurring failures, maintenance intervals, software updates and customer urgency around downtime.
- Public evidence needed: installed base; service headcount; locations; service contracts; repair volumes; service revenue/margin; renewal rates.
- Monetization model: contracts, warranty extensions, service plans, paid repairs, support subscriptions, modernization.
- Pricing power likelihood: High if OEM controls diagnostics, parts, manuals, software keys, training and warranty validity.
- No-go warning signs: no service revenue visibility; customers self-maintain; OEM response time weak; service not tied to uptime or renewal.
- Current evidence level: E4-E5

### Independent system integrator service
- What it does: Maintains and troubleshoots robot cells, EOAT, PLCs, safety devices, conveyors and warehouse automation around OEM robots.
- Why it may bottleneck: Many sites are mixed-vendor and customer-specific; local integrator availability can determine downtime duration.
- Public evidence needed: repeat service accounts; technician certifications; emergency response contracts; mixed-vendor capability; customer case studies.
- Monetization model: time-and-materials, retainers, emergency support, PM contracts, retrofit/recommissioning projects.
- Pricing power likelihood: Medium: local trust and mixed-vendor knowledge matter, but labor can commoditize.
- No-go warning signs: only one-off project work; low margin labor; no repeat accounts; no safety or OEM certification.
- Current evidence level: E3-E4

### Spare parts / warehouse logistics
- What it does: Stocks and dispatches motors, reducers, controllers, teach pendants, batteries, cables, sensors, safety scanners, obsolete parts and refurbished modules.
- Why it may bottleneck: Robot downtime can be extremely expensive; long-tail parts and obsolete models create inventory and logistics barriers.
- Public evidence needed: parts SKU count; obsolete support; dispatch speed; service warehouses; parts revenue; fill rates; remanufacturing volumes.
- Monetization model: OEM parts sales, refurbished parts, service kits, consignment inventory, urgent dispatch fees.
- Pricing power likelihood: High for proprietary/obsolete/safety-critical parts; lower for commodity consumables.
- No-go warning signs: parts easily sourced from generic suppliers; low inventory turns; warranty or safety issues make refurb risky.
- Current evidence level: E4-E5

### Uptime monitoring software
- What it does: Collects alarms, utilization, robot health, maintenance status, backups and fleet dashboards.
- Why it may bottleneck: Fleet scale makes manual monitoring insufficient; service teams need triage and predictive alerts.
- Public evidence needed: connected robot count; paid subscriptions; reduced downtime; integration with technician dispatch; renewal rates.
- Monetization model: subscription, bundled service contract, paid portal, fleet-management module.
- Pricing power likelihood: Medium: high if tied to OEM diagnostics and service workflows; weak if only visualization.
- No-go warning signs: dashboard-only claims; no paid adoption; no downtime reduction; no link to service dispatch.
- Current evidence level: E3-E4

### Predictive maintenance
- What it does: Uses condition data to predict reducer, servo, battery, lubrication, process or safety failures before downtime.
- Why it may bottleneck: Predictive alerts can shift service from emergency repair to planned downtime and reduce spare-parts surprises.
- Public evidence needed: failure prediction accuracy; planned-maintenance conversion; customer case studies; avoided downtime; service revenue.
- Monetization model: software subscription, premium service plan, analytics module, condition-monitoring service.
- Pricing power likelihood: Medium-high if models require proprietary robot data; low if generic analytics.
- No-go warning signs: false positives; no measurable downtime reduction; customers ignore alerts; no monetization.
- Current evidence level: E3-E4

### Calibration / testing service
- What it does: Verifies robot accuracy, metrology, vision, scanners, end effectors and process repeatability after installation or change.
- Why it may bottleneck: Robots drift, crash, relocate, change tools and operate in messy physical environments where accuracy affects scrap and uptime.
- Public evidence needed: calibration frequency; acceptance-test records; metrology service revenue; customer case studies; scrap/downtime reduction.
- Monetization model: service visits, calibration certificates, metrology equipment, QA software, recommissioning projects.
- Pricing power likelihood: Medium: specialist tools and expertise matter, but routine calibration may commoditize.
- No-go warning signs: no repeat cycles; customers accept lower accuracy; calibration tools commoditize; no production impact proof.
- Current evidence level: E3-E4

### Safety inspection / compliance service
- What it does: Validates guards, scanners, safety controllers, stop functions, procedures, documentation and standards compliance.
- Why it may bottleneck: Safety issues can block operation or expose customers to legal and worker-safety risk; standards apply across lifecycle.
- Public evidence needed: standard references; safety validation reports; inspection frequency; compliance service contracts; training and audit records.
- Monetization model: inspection fees, validation projects, safety retrofits, training, documentation packages.
- Pricing power likelihood: Medium-high where standards knowledge and liability matter; lower if internal EHS teams own it.
- No-go warning signs: inspection reduced to low-cost paperwork; no recurring cadence; customers internalize compliance.
- Current evidence level: E3


## 6. Disconfirmation Checklist

See `field_service_disconfirmation_checklist.csv` for row-level tests. Cross-cutting disconfirmers:
- customers maintain robot fleets internally without OEM tools, parts or paid service;
- spare parts are generic and easily substituted;
- uptime software does not reduce downtime or service cost;
- service gross margin is weak after travel, warranty, inventory and technician labor;
- installed-base growth does not create service revenue growth;
- safety inspection is handled as routine internal paperwork;
- humanoid-specific service claims do not progress beyond pilots.

## 8. Final Decision

Decision: field service / spare parts / uptime monitoring is a valid Serenity-style bottleneck lane.

Proceed to R3 supplier-category screening: YES, but only with public-only, category-first screening. Do not jump directly to company selection. R3 should rank subsegments by evidence quality, service-economics visibility, installed-base attachment, pricing power and mapping purity.

Evidence required before company-level screening:
- service revenue, aftersales revenue, software maintenance/support revenue or spare-parts revenue disclosure;
- installed base, connected robot count, service attach rate or renewal-rate proxy;
- service gross margin, cash-flow contribution or warranty-burden evidence;
- technician footprint, parts warehouse footprint, repair volumes or dispatch-time evidence;
- customer case studies showing uptime improvement or downtime reduction;
- standards or certification evidence for safety inspection and recommissioning;
- proof that predictive maintenance is paid and operational, not just dashboard marketing.

No-go areas:
- humanoid service TAM without installed fleets;
- predictive maintenance claims with no paid adoption;
- broad automation companies where service exposure is too diluted;
- local labor-only service shops without certification or recurring contracts;
- spare parts where generic substitutes remove pricing power;
- customer concentration mistaken for broad adoption.
